<p><strong>A confidentiality agreement and a non-disclosure agreement (NDA)</strong> both protect sensitive information from unauthorized disclosure, but they are not identical. The core difference is directionality: a confidentiality agreement is typically mutual, binding both parties to secrecy, while an <a href="https://www.pandadoc.com/ask/how-to-sign-an-nda/">NDA</a> is usually unilateral, restricting only the receiving party. </p>



<p>In practice, a mutual NDA and a confidentiality agreement serve the same function.</p>



<p>The terms are used interchangeably in many business contexts, and courts generally interpret these documents based on their clauses, not their titles. Understanding the structural distinction helps you choose the right document, draft stronger protections, and avoid common mistakes that weaken enforceability.</p>



<p>This article covers the difference between the two, when to use each one, what every agreement should include, what happens if one is breached, and how to create either document using a template.</p>





<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="1"><strong>What is a confidentiality agreement?</strong></h2>



<p>A confidentiality agreement is a legally binding contract that obligates all signing parties to keep specified information secret and not use it for unauthorized purposes. Unlike a standard NDA, a confidentiality agreement is typically mutual — meaning both parties share sensitive information and both parties are bound to protect it.</p>



<p>Confidentiality agreements can exist as standalone documents or as clauses embedded within a larger contract, such as a services agreement or a partnership agreement. They are most common in situations where the information exchange flows in both directions.</p>



<p><strong>Common scenarios where confidentiality agreements are used:</strong></p>



<p>•  Joint ventures and co-development projects where both parties contribute proprietary technology or processes</p>



<p>•  OEM and supplier negotiations where both sides share manufacturing methods, pricing models, or client lists</p>



<p>•  Business partnership discussions where both parties disclose commercially sensitive information before signing a formal contract</p>



<p>•  Merger and acquisition due diligence where both companies open their books</p>



<p>The scope of protection typically covers trade secrets, client lists, intellectual property, pricing models, and proprietary processes. Duration is specified in the agreement itself — most range from two to five years, though perpetual terms are common when trade secrets are involved.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="2"><strong>What is a non-disclosure agreement (NDA)?</strong></h2>



<p>A non-disclosure agreement is a legally enforceable contract that creates a confidential relationship between two parties and restricts the unauthorized disclosure of sensitive information. NDAs are technically a subset of confidentiality agreements — the key practical distinction is that they are typically unilateral, meaning only one party is disclosing information and only the receiving party is bound to secrecy.</p>



<p>NDAs can be unilateral (one-way) or mutual (two-way). When an NDA is mutual, it is functionally equivalent to a confidentiality agreement. The document’s title does not change its legal effect — courts interpret the substance of the clauses, not the name on the cover page.</p>



<p>You may also see the term CDA (Confidential Disclosure Agreement), which is the same concept under a different name. CDAs are particularly common in pharmaceutical and biotech industries.</p>



<p><strong>Common scenarios where NDAs are used:</strong></p>



<p>•  Employee or contractor onboarding where the new hire gains access to trade secrets, client lists, or internal systems</p>



<p>•  Sharing a product roadmap or proprietary technology with a potential investor before a funding round</p>



<p>•  Protecting a patentable idea before filing a patent application — public disclosure can waive patent rights in most jurisdictions</p>



<p>•  Early-stage vendor or agency engagements where your data is at risk but the other party is not disclosing comparable information</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Unilateral vs mutual NDA: understanding the key types</strong></h2>



<p>The distinction between unilateral and mutual NDAs is the structural foundation that the entire confidentiality agreement vs NDA comparison rests on. Understanding it makes choosing the right document straightforward.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Unilateral NDA</strong></h3>



<p>A unilateral NDA involves one disclosing party and one receiving party. Only the receiving party is bound to secrecy. This is the most common type of NDA and the version most people picture when they hear the term.</p>



<p><strong>When to use it:</strong></p>



<p>•  An employer onboarding a new hire who will access internal systems and trade secrets</p>



<p>•  A startup sharing its business plan with a potential investor</p>



<p>•  A company granting a contractor access to proprietary processes or client data</p>



<p>PandaDoc offers a dedicated <a href="https://www.pandadoc.com/employee-non-disclosure-agreement/">employee NDA template</a> designed for exactly these scenarios.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Mutual (bilateral) NDA</strong></h3>



<p>A mutual NDA binds both parties equally. Both share sensitive information, and both are restricted from disclosing it. A mutual NDA is functionally equivalent to a confidentiality agreement — the difference is largely one of naming convention and industry preference.</p>



<p><strong>When to use it:</strong></p>



<p>•  Joint ventures where both companies contribute proprietary technology</p>



<p>•  M&amp;A discussions where both sides open their financials and operations</p>



<p>•  OEM and supplier relationships where manufacturing processes are shared in both directions</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>When to use a confidentiality agreement</strong></h2>



<p>Use a confidentiality agreement when all parties have sensitive information they need to protect and the information exchange is genuinely two-way. If both sides bear equal confidentiality risk, a mutual agreement is the right choice.</p>



<p><strong>Typical situations:</strong></p>



<p>•  Two companies entering a joint venture and sharing proprietary processes with each other</p>



<p>•  An OEM and a supplier exchanging manufacturing specifications and pricing models</p>



<p>•  Business partners disclosing financial data and strategic plans during pre-contract negotiations</p>



<p>•  Co-development projects where both parties contribute intellectual property</p>



<p>In many of these situations, the confidentiality language is embedded as a clause within a larger agreement rather than drafted as a standalone document. Either format is legally valid — the key is that the terms are clear, specific, and signed by both parties.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>When to use an NDA</strong></h2>



<p>Use an NDA when only one party is disclosing sensitive information and the flow of confidential data is one-way. The receiving party is bound to secrecy, but the disclosing party has no equivalent obligation because they are not receiving anything sensitive in return.</p>



<p><strong>Typical situations:</strong></p>



<p>•  Onboarding a new employee or contractor who will access trade secrets, client lists, or internal systems</p>



<p>•  Sharing proprietary technology or a product roadmap with a potential investor before a funding round</p>



<p>•  Protecting a patentable idea before a patent application is filed — public disclosure can waive patent rights in most jurisdictions</p>



<p>•  Engaging a vendor or agency that will handle your data but is not disclosing comparable information in return</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>What to include in either agreement</strong></h2>



<p>Whether you are drafting a confidentiality agreement or an NDA, the core components are the same. A well-structured agreement should include all of the following:</p>



<p><strong>1. Definition of confidential information</strong></p>



<p>Be specific. Vague language creates loopholes and weakens enforceability. List the categories of information being protected — trade secrets, financial data, client lists, product designs, proprietary processes — rather than relying on a blanket statement.</p>



<p><strong>2. Identification of all parties</strong></p>



<p>Use full legal names, not just trade names. A common and costly mistake is identifying a party by a DBA name that does not match their registered legal entity. This can create enforcement problems if the agreement is ever challenged.</p>



<p><strong>3. Permitted use clause</strong></p>



<p>Define what the receiving party is allowed to do with the information and for what specific purpose. Without this clause, the scope of permitted use is ambiguous — which benefits the receiving party, not the disclosing party.</p>



<p><strong>4. Duration of the confidentiality obligation</strong></p>



<p>Specify whether the obligation has a defined term (typically two to five years) or is perpetual. Trade secrets often warrant a perpetual clause because their value depends on continued secrecy.</p>



<p><strong>5. Carve-outs and exclusions</strong></p>



<p>Not all information can or should be covered. Standard exclusions include information that is already in the public domain, information the receiving party developed independently, and information the receiving party is legally compelled to disclose by court order or government request. Omitting carve-outs can make an agreement unenforceable.</p>



<p><strong>6. Consequences of breach</strong></p>



<p>Specify the remedies available to the injured party — compensatory damages, injunctive relief, or both. Including a remedies clause strengthens the agreement’s deterrent effect and gives the injured party a clearer path to enforcement.</p>



<p><strong>7. Jurisdiction and governing law</strong></p>



<p>State which jurisdiction’s laws govern the agreement and where disputes will be resolved. This is particularly important for agreements between parties in different states or countries.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>What happens if a confidentiality agreement or NDA is breached?</strong></h2>



<p>When a party violates a confidentiality agreement or NDA, the injured party has several legal remedies available. The specific consequences depend on the terms of the agreement, the jurisdiction, and the nature of the breach.</p>



<p><strong>Civil liability and damages</strong></p>



<p>The injured party can file a civil lawsuit seeking compensatory damages — the financial losses directly caused by the unauthorized disclosure. If the breach resulted in lost revenue, competitive harm, or diminished trade secret value, those losses can be quantified and claimed.</p>



<p><strong>Injunctive relief</strong></p>



<p>Courts can issue an injunction ordering the breaching party to stop disclosing the information immediately. This is often the most urgent remedy because ongoing disclosure compounds the damage.</p>



<p><strong>Federal trade secret protection</strong></p>



<p>In the United States, the <strong>Defend Trade Secrets Act (DTSA, 18 U.S.C. § 1836)</strong> provides a federal cause of action for trade secret misappropriation. This means the injured party can bring a case in federal court regardless of where the breach occurred, as long as the trade secret is related to a product or service used in interstate or international commerce.</p>



<p><strong>State-level enforcement</strong></p>



<p>Most states have adopted some version of the Uniform Trade Secrets Act (UTSA), though the specific provisions vary. Notably, New York has not adopted the UTSA and instead relies on common law protections. For a full list of state adoptions, see the <a href="https://www.uniformlaws.org/committees/community-home?CommunityKey=3a2538fb-e030-4e2d-a9e2-90373dc05b92">Uniform Law Commission</a>.</p>



<p><strong>Limits on enforceability</strong></p>



<p>Courts can and do invalidate NDAs that are overly broad, unreasonably burdensome, or drafted to conceal illegal activity. An NDA cannot be used to prevent whistleblowing or to block disclosures that are legally required by a government authority or court order.</p>



<p>For high-stakes situations involving significant financial exposure or complex jurisdictional issues, consult a licensed attorney.</p>
